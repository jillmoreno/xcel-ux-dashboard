Stage Their pace

Study plan — let them start, then ask for a date

A 40-hour Texas Life & Health pre-licensing course is roughly a 30-day piece of work, and the learner who just bought it does not yet know that. Asking them on day zero to commit to 5 credit hours a week asks for a number they have no basis to pick — so this explores the opposite order: let them study first, watch how fast they actually go, and then make one concrete offer built from their own rate.

Two demo axes above. Stage moves the learner through the pre-plan window and re-renders the goal surface. Their pace changes the observed minutes-per-credit-hour, and every date on this page recomputes from it — which is the claim being tested: that a plan offer is only worth making once it is theirs.

1It asks for the derived number and derives the real one. Nobody walks in knowing whether 5 credit hours a week is a lot. Almost everybody walks in with a date — a start date at an agency, an exam window, "before the holidays". The date is the fact the learner owns; the pace is the thing we should be working out for them. Here the date is the fifth and smallest option, in a card that shows no result.
2The presets are labelled by ambition, and priced in weeks. "Aggressive / Recommended / Relaxed" grades the learner before they have done anything, and 19 weeks is not a unit anyone feels. What can actually be judged is a Tuesday: about an hour a night, five nights, done by 16 October.
3There is no second half. The card is a first-run moment with no model of what happens once the learner is behind it — which is the state a plan spends most of its life in. Section 05 is that half.
410 / 19 / 48 weeks is a renewal-cycle frame. It fits continuing education, where the deadline is a licence expiry two years out. Pre-licensing is a sprint with an external exam at the end of it, so the unit is days and the finish line is being ready to sit, not being done.

The one-line version. Keep the three-preset shape; invert what it asks for. The learner picks when; we quote what it costs per evening; either side can be edited and the other follows.

02

Two objects, deliberately kept apart

Most of the confusion in this area comes from one object doing both jobs. Separating them is what makes every later state answerable — in particular the one where the learner is behind, because then you can ask which of the two moves instead of quietly sliding both.

Learner-owned · rarely changes

The Goal

  • One date: when they want to sit the state exam.
  • Comes from outside the product — a job start, a licence window, an agency deadline.
  • Changing it is a decision, and is always explicit.
  • It is the only thing we ask the learner to choose.
System-owned · re-derived freely

The Plan

  • A pace: which evenings, and how long each one.
  • Derived from the goal, the hours left, and their observed rate.
  • Re-derived silently whenever reality moves. No confirmation needed.
  • It is what the dashboard, the calendar and Today's Tasks all read.

Why the exam date and the completion date are two different facts, in two different colours. Finishing the coursework is a primary event — it is ours, it is data, we control it. Sitting the licensing exam is a brand-red event — external, scheduled by PSI, and red's one semantic everywhere else in this product. A picker that returns a single date has already lost that distinction. This one returns both, and the gap between them is EXAM_BUFFER_INVENTED.

03

The pre-plan window — four stages, none of them on a timer

The ask was to let people start studying before being pushed into a plan. The trap is implementing that as a countdown — "nag on day 7" — because a countdown nags the learner who has done nothing (and has no basis to answer) while leaving alone the one who did four chapters in a weekend (and would love an answer). Every trigger below is earned progress, not elapsed time.

The 30-day finding, stated plainly: in a course that is itself about 30 days long, you cannot spend 30 days unplanned. A grace window has to be a fraction of the course, and the natural fraction is not a number of days at all — it is until we know how fast they go, which on the pace axis above lands somewhere between the first chapter and the second. That is the window.

Stage 3 is the only one that is not dismissible, and only in one case. If a hard external deadline exists — course access expiring, an agency-set date, a licence window — the card stays. But it arrives pre-filled with that deadline, so the learner is choosing whether to beat it, not whether to have a goal at all. Absent a hard deadline, even stage 3 can be dismissed; it just returns. There is no blocking modal anywhere in this flow.

04

The picker — date and pace on one surface, each recalculating the other

Three presets priced in evenings, a date field as a peer rather than a fifth card, and a live readout of both dates. Type a date and the presets re-rank against it; pick a preset and the date field fills in. Everything here is computed from the demo axes above — change Their pace and watch every number move.

The impossible date is the case the reference has no answer for. Ask for a sit date nine days out on 34 remaining hours and something has to give. Refusing the date is wrong — some learners really do have a Friday deadline and really will do nothing else that week. Accepting it silently is worse. So the picker quotes the cost and lets them take it, and only calls a date unreachable past CEILING_MINS_INVENTED, where the arithmetic stops being a pace and starts being a fiction.

05

After the plan — five states, and what each is allowed to say

A plan is a promise with a comparison built into it, so the interesting design is not the promise — it is the tone ladder. The single most important row here is the second one, where the correct behaviour is to say nothing at all.

Why "slightly behind" must be silent. A plan that raises its voice for one missed Tuesday teaches the learner that its voice means nothing, and by the time it has something real to say they have stopped reading it. The tolerance is the design, not an oversight — DRIFT_TOLERANCE_INVENTED exists so that the first alert a learner ever sees is one worth acting on.

Never slide the date silently. When the goal and the plan can no longer both be true, both options are put on screen with their price attached — keep the date, here is the new evening versus keep the evening, here is the new date. A product that quietly moves the finish line is the reason learners stop believing completion estimates.

07

Open, and known to be

Whose deadline wins

An agency buys the seat and sets a date; the learner sets their own, earlier or later. Two goals on one course. Neither "agency always wins" nor "learner always wins" is obviously right, and the admin roster already has an opinion about who is at risk — which would then be measuring against a date the learner never agreed to.

Study days, or all seven

The presets here price evenings and weekends separately, which is honest but means the plan has a shape — and a shape can be violated by someone who studies fine, just on the wrong days. Does the plan care which evenings, or only how many minutes a week?

Whether we can quote an exam date at all

The buffer between finishing and sitting is a made-up seven days. Real PSI availability varies by state and by month, and a quoted sit-date we cannot honour is worse than no sit-date. This may have to become a range, or a link.

Where the goal lives on the dashboard

Stage 2 puts a card in the dashboard's second slot — the slot the recommended band occupies in classic, and the slot editorial deletes outright. The goal nudge and the recommended band cannot both be the second thing.
