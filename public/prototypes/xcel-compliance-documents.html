Compliance documents: six shapes, 24 files

XCEL Solutions · the _XCEL-PE Affidavits and EAs set · all 24 read 2026-10-20

Every compliance document XCEL attaches to a pre-licensing course — affidavits, attestations, notices and enrollment agreements — read from the source files and sorted into the six content shapes they actually fall into. The claim this document makes is that they do not fit one fixed flow, and that the right unit of reuse is the shape, not the document.

23 of 24 are now verbatim from the source files, and every shape is confirmed against its state profile. The exception is Minnesota’s Post Final Affidavit, whose PDF cannot be read — see below. One further passage, the Spanish half of Florida’s Property & Casualty notice, is a correction rather than a transcription, and is flagged where it appears.

The problem

The approved visual style was built once as PreCourseRequirementsWizard — a fixed three-step flow: Confirm Name → Confirm License → Affidavit. It is a good flow for the document it was drawn against. It is the wrong flow for most of the set.

Across the 24 files the step counts run from one to three, the fields differ per state, and four documents have a second signer entirely — an exam monitor with their own declaration, their own identity fields, and a relationship-to-examinee question whose options are not the same in any two states. Building each as its own modal gives six components that drift apart; threading conditionals through one gives a component nobody can safely change. The third option is the one taken here: one shell, and the shape decides what it renders.

Shape is not the same question as kind

Each document also has a compliance type — the name the business uses for it. The two cut across each other in both directions, which is why they are two fields rather than one:

  • Post Final Affidavit spans two shapes — four are two-party, three are single-signer.
  • affidavit-single spans three types — Pre-Final, Post Final and Ohio’s combined Final Affidavit are structurally identical and administratively different.

What reading them actually turned up

Six findings. None were visible from the file names — each came out of opening the document, and the first one inverted a core assumption.

1. The two-party shape was assigned to exactly the wrong documents

The brief named California, Illinois and Ohio as the three affidavits with a proctor. None of them has one. All three are a single declaration, a signature and a date; what the brief read as a proctor is a roster-upload block — the identity details the education provider must report after completion.

Meanwhile Colorado, Connecticut, Wisconsin and West Virginia — all four filed as single-signer — each carry a full “To be completed and signed by Exam Monitor” section: a relationship question, proctor identity fields, a proctor declaration and a proctor signature.

Every document was in the wrong half. Three built for a signer they do not have; four missing the signer they do. This is the finding that most changes the component, and it would have shipped invisibly — the flow runs perfectly either way, it just collects the wrong things from the wrong people.

2. The relationship lists are not shared, and never were

Colorado, Connecticut and West Virginia offer Co-Worker, Librarian and Licensed Agent. Wisconsin offers Co-Worker, Librarian and Neighbor. One hardcoded list would be wrong in one state out of four, silently. The proctor’s own fields vary too: Wisconsin wants a name, address and phone; West Virginia wants only a name.

3. Florida’s Property & Casualty notice really is broken

The brief reported its Spanish as copy-pasted from the Life & Health notice. It is. The English correctly names FAIA and the Florida General Lines, Adjuster and Industrial Fire Study Manual; the Spanish directly beneath it names NAIFA and the Life, Health and Variable Annuity manual. A Spanish-speaking Property & Casualty student is told to buy the wrong book from the wrong association.

This is the one passage in the set that is not a transcription: the Spanish here is a translation of the document’s own English, so the two halves finally agree. It should be confirmed before it replaces the live file.

4. The placeholders had the wrong subject, not just the wrong words

Every stand-in text checked described the wrong thing entirely. Florida’s Life & Health notice is about the NAIFA study manual, not the education requirement. The XCEL Generic T&C is about proctoring, not attendance and refunds. Illinois’ T&C is the state’s course-hours rule. California’s is a 12-hour ethics and anti-fraud requirement with study-time tracking and a 20-minute inactivity timeout — none of which any summary would guess.

The lesson generalises: placeholder text reads as finished. A document with the wrong subject is more dangerous than an empty one, because nothing about it looks unresolved.

5. Minnesota’s Post Final Affidavit still cannot be read

It is the only PDF among 23 Word files, built with Adobe Fill & Sign, and its fonts are subset with no ToUnicode map and no /Differences array — nothing in the file says which character each glyph stands for. Text extraction, raw stream decoding and a character-map search all fail for the same reason. Enough partially decodes to suggest a two-party form (“Student’s Full Legal Name / License Number”, then a proctor block), but that is inference from recovered glyphs, not a read, and it is recorded as such. It needs opening in a viewer and copying out by hand.

6. Florida has no post-exam affidavit, and that broke the data model

Florida requires a pre-exam unassisted acknowledgment. Its profile is explicit about the other end: “After the final exam: proctor or attestation: No regulation.” The catalog had a two-value field — before the course, or after the final — and neither was true of Florida. It had been filed as post-final, which said the opposite of what the state asks for.

So the field is now the PRD’s own list of moments, and the spread shows why two values were never going to hold: five documents at checkout, three before the course opens, two before the first lesson, five before the exam, seven after it, two at completion. Four of those moments sat inside one value before.

One moment carries no document on purpose — during the exam, where a proctor is present and checks identity but nothing is signed. It is in the list so the set is the regulation’s rather than a shape fitted to the files we happen to hold.

7. Two documents the set does not contain

Reading all nineteen state profiles turned up two requirements with no file behind them, both proctor affidavits:

  • Mississippi. After the exam “the proctor signs an affidavit … and gives their name, address and phone number to the provider. It may be administered and signed electronically (state template).” The only Mississippi file in the set is a student pre-exam acknowledgment — which the state does not ask for. The form that matters is a state template nobody has pulled.
  • Minnesota. Its proctor affidavit is the PDF that cannot be read. Minnesota is also explicitly silent on pre-exam attestation, so the Minnesota pre-final document we do hold is XCEL’s own practice, not a state rule.

8. Identity fields were being collected nowhere

Four affidavits name identity fields the state’s roster needs — California’s date of birth and last four of SSN, Illinois’ full SSN, Ohio’s exam date, phone and SSN, New York’s exam date. The component rendered them only on the two-party student step, so on every single-signer affidavit they were silently dropped: the flow completed, the learner signed, and nothing was asked. Fixed; they now gate the identity step, because a roster the state rejects is not a completion.

New York’s is the one that was also wrong in the data. Its profile asks for “name, date the exam was taken, signature”; the source document’s field is headed “Today’s Date”, which is the day the affidavit is signed. The same day in the happy path, and a different one the moment an affidavit is completed late.

9. The paper order is not the filling order

All four two-party forms print the student’s block above the monitor’s, so the shape ran student first. Wisconsin’s profile describes the opposite in practice: the proctor verifies identity and completes the Affidavit of Personal Responsibility before the exam, and both parties sign after it. Following the paper would have asked a learner to attest before the person meant to have verified them had.

Wisconsin now opens on the proctor; Colorado, Connecticut and West Virginia are untouched, because nothing says they differ. The check that a proctor cannot be the examinee had to become symmetrical to survive it — read only from the proctor’s side, it would have compared against an empty name and passed every time, leaving the learner free to type the monitor’s name with nothing to catch it.

10. Three smaller things, for whoever owns the files

  • MN PE-Terms and Conditions…- Copy.docx carries Windows’ duplicate suffix with no original beside it — but its text is not a duplicate of any other row, so this looks like a lost original rather than a stray.
  • Ohio’s combined affidavit explains itself: “OH rule says both can’t be on one form and OCD does not have the functionality to attach 2 post final affidavits.” That is why a sixth type name exists.
  • Oregon’s enrollment agreement ends by asking the student to type their ethnicity as free text. Not rendered here — a demographic question collected that way is a data-protection decision, not a layout one.

Seeing it work

The live component renders every one of the 24 from this data — one shell, reconfigured per shape. Open the picker and choose any document. The shapes worth looking at first are a Florida notice (bilingual, no signer), Ohio’s Registration Form (signature, four identity fields), Wisconsin’s Post Final (two signers, and the only Neighbor option), and Minnesota’s Post Final (the unreadable one, which cannot be completed).

Source of truth: src/data/complianceDocuments.ts holds all 24 rows with their wording and the file each was read from. This page argues the structure; that file is the content.
