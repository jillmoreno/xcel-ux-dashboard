The Study Pace card — 21 ways to say the same four facts

The shipped tile says four things: how heavy an evening is, how many evenings a week, how far through the window that lands you, and the date it finishes. Every variation below says exactly those four things — none of them invents a figure, and all 21 read the same model as Pace presets and the live StudyPaceTile. Change an axis in the bar and all 21 re-derive together, which is the only honest way to compare them: a layout that reads beautifully at 1¾ hours can fall apart at 3 hours · won't fit, and the second case is the one that decides.

Four questions, one per section. 01 — layout: what shape holds four facts in a 186px tile. 02 — emphasis: which of the four gets the big type, which is a product decision about what the learner came to find out. 03 — the graphic: what, if anything, the bar should be. 04 — interaction: whether the tile stays a statement or becomes a control. Section 05 puts the finalists at real width side by side, and 06 scores them.

01

Layout & density

Same four facts, same emphasis, six shapes. The constraint that kills most of these is 186px — the tile's real width in the four-up band — and the second is that the chip, the number and the finish line all have a long state: "Full window · heavy", "1¾ hours", "Finishes by Dec 30". Anything that only works at the short state is not a layout, it is a screenshot.

The compact row (B) is the one to argue about. It drops the bar and the chip and fits on one line — which is exactly right for a mobile list or a secondary rail, and exactly wrong as the only place the learner ever sees their pace, because "1¾ hours a night" with no window behind it is a demand rather than a plan. Treat B as a second rendering of the same component, not a replacement.

02

Data emphasis — which fact gets the big type

One layout, four different lead figures. This is the section with an actual product decision in it, because the big number answers a different question in each: A answers "what does tonight cost me", B "when am I done", C "how much runway is left", D "what am I signing up for this week". They are not interchangeable — a learner who is behind wants C, a learner deciding whether to start tonight wants A, and a learner who booked an exam wants B.

C is the only one that can be read as a threat. "23 days left" is the same fact as "finishes by May 29" with the reassurance stripped out, and on a card the learner did not ask for, the countdown framing is what turns a plan into a nag. If C ships anywhere it should be inside the at-risk state, not as the default rendering — which is an argument for keeping the emphasis a prop rather than a variant.

03

Visual treatment — what the graphic is for

The shipped bar encodes chosen finish ÷ days to the ceiling — how much of the window the plan uses. That is a real quantity and it belongs on the card. But a bar is not the only way to draw it, and two of the options below encode a different quantity entirely: the week strip (C) and the evening bars (F) draw the shape of the week, which is the thing a learner is actually agreeing to. Every option here keeps the date printed in words, so nothing is carried by colour alone.

E — no graphic — is in this list seriously. At 186px the bar is six pixels tall and about ninety wide; it survives as texture rather than as a reading. If the number and the date carry the whole message, the honest question is whether the bar is decoration that costs a row of vertical space the finish sentence would rather have. The counter-argument is the at-risk case, where a nearly-full bar is the fastest possible read — so this is a question about which state you optimise for.

04

Interaction model — statement, or control panel

The shipped tile operates nothing: one button, Adjust, and every control lives in the sheet behind it. That was a deliberate call, and these four are the cases against it. All of them are live — change them and the tile re-derives from the same model, which is the point: the question is not whether inline controls work, it is what they cost the dashboard around them.

Count the controls, not the clicks. B and C each save one tap, and each turns a tile in a four-up band into something the learner has to read and decide about before they can start studying — beside three other tiles doing the same, that is a dashboard nobody scans. D is the compromise that survives scrutiny: the card is a statement until the learner asks it not to be, and the expanded state is the sheet's content rendered in place. E is the one to be most careful with — dragging a finish date is delightful once and unusable on a phone, and it has no keyboard story that isn't just a slider.

05

The finalists at real width — 186px, four up

Everything above is shown at ~258px, which flatters all of it. This is the width the tile actually gets in the dashboard band. Scroll the rail: the failures here are text wrapping to three lines, the chip colliding with the number, and the footer's two links stacking.

This rail is the test that matters and it is worth re-running it on every axis — particularly Work left: 40 hours with Access left: 12 days, which is the "won't fit" state, and 9 hours / 60 days, where the chip reads "Relaxed" and the evening drops to a figure so small the big type looks silly.

What each one costs

Scored against the four things that actually decide it: does it hold at 186px, does it survive the won't-fit state, does it stay a statement (nothing to operate), and does it need new data the model does not already produce.

My read: keep 01·A's shape, take 03·C's week dots in place of the linear bar (same vertical cost, encodes the commitment rather than the ratio), and add 04·D's disclosure so Adjust has a cheap in-place form for the one change learners make most — nights a week — while the sheet keeps the exam date and the plan. That is one new sub-component, no new data, and the tile is still a statement when nobody touches it.

Every figure on this page derives. Hours of work remaining, the access window and the study-style multiplier are the three demo axes; everything else — nights a week, minutes an evening, the finish date, the chip, the fill percentage — comes out of the same functions the product uses (src/lib/studyPace.ts), re-implemented here rather than imported because this is a standalone document. The one authored thing is the date: "today" is pinned to 11 May 2026 so the finish dates in these variations do not drift as the page ages, and so the default reproduces the card in the brief exactly — 1¾ hours, 5 nights, finishes by May 29.

What this page does not answer. It does not touch the Readiness tile beside it, and the two are related: several of these layouts would look wrong next to a Readiness tile drawn a different way, and that pairing has to be settled as a band rather than a tile. It also shows only the recommended preset's states — easy, ok, heavy and no — and not the learner-adjusted labels, which the sibling page covers.
