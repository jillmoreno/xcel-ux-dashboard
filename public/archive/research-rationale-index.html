Home / Dashboard

The learner's landing surface — the first thing shown after sign-in. Decisions here are about what earns the most valuable screen space, how the page greets a returning learner, and how the hero and recommendation surfaces are composed.

Open question · Decision 🔗Copy link

Should we include another welcome message on the actual Home page?

Recommendation

No — keep a single greeting, and place it in the left nav. A second verbatim "Welcome back, Sarah" at the top of Home is redundant and spends prime space on low-value content. Lead the Home page with the learner's Current Learning Path instead.

Context

The persistent left nav already shows "Welcome back, Sarah Cook" with the learner's avatar, membership (Passport), and profession/state. The open proposal is to add a second welcome banner across the top of the Home page. The concern raised: that reads as unneeded duplication. The research supports that concern on two fronts.

Why — the reasoning

  1. Duplication has a real cognitive cost, not just an aesthetic one.

    NN/g's work on redundancy found that when the same element appears twice on a page, users spend effort deciding whether the duplicate is the same thing or something new. The recommended fix is almost never "keep both" — it's to make the single instance more prominent or remove the distractor. The one sanctioned exception is redundancy that opens a genuinely different navigation path; repeating the same greeting doesn't clear that bar.

  2. A greeting is low-value content sitting in your highest-value space.

    The top of Home is prime real estate. NN/g's prioritization principle is blunt: "If everything is prominent, then nothing is." For this screen the high-value content is the Current Learning Path card (63% complete, Mandatory 6/9, license expires 12/15/2026) — what a returning learner came to check. A greeting banner pushes that down.

  3. The nav greeting already does the identity job.

    Confirming "you're signed in as Sarah, Passport member, Nursing · Florida" is the useful work a greeting does — and the left nav does it persistently, alongside the account context it belongs with. A page-level repeat adds no new information.

Nuance — when a Home greeting would earn its space

A page-level greeting is justified only if it does something the nav one doesn't: a time-aware, motivational line tied to a next action.

e.g. "Good morning, Sarah — you're 3 courses from renewal. Pick up where you left off →"

A plain repeat of "Welcome back, Sarah" is not that. If the goal is warmth, make it contextual and action-oriented — otherwise cut it.

What we recommend

  • Keep the single identity greeting in the left nav, where it doubles as account/membership context.
  • Lead the Home page with the Current Learning Path — the content a returning learner actually wants.
  • If warmth is desired, replace the static welcome with a contextual, action-oriented line, not a second "Welcome back."

Open question · Decision 🔗Copy link

What product-card style works best on a Home page — large data-rich cards, or smaller shelf-style cards for skimming?

Recommendation

Default to smaller shelf-style cards on Home. The Home page's job is quick discovery and re-engagement — skimming, not evaluating. Compact cards fit more options per row and read as "browse me." Reserve the large, data-rich cards for the surface where people actually decide: the Course Catalog and search.

Context

Home surfaces like Recommended, Jump Back In, and What's New are discovery zones — the learner is scanning to find something worth clicking, not comparing a shortlist in detail. The platform already supports both treatments (the Recommended band's shelf ≈172px vs. large ≈262px cards), so this is choosing the right default per surface, not new work.

The trade-off at a glance

✓ Best for Home
Shelf / compact cards

Discovery & skimming

  • More options visible per row
  • Lower per-card cognitive load
  • Cover + title + a meta cue is enough to click
  • Themed rows chunk the choice
Large / data-rich cards

Comparison & decision

  • Room for price, credits, rating, format
  • Best with a few high-consideration items
  • Right home: Catalog, search, a focused promo
  • On Home they crowd out breadth

Why — the reasoning

  1. Match the card to the task: browse vs. decide.

    A Home learner is deciding what to look at, not purchasing on the spot. A full spec sheet on every tile answers a question they haven't asked yet.

  2. Fewer, denser choices scan faster (Hick's Law).

    Decision time rises with the number and complexity of options. Large cards limit how many fit above the fold and pack each with more to process. Compact cards in labeled shelves cut perceived choice into digestible chunks.

  3. Give each card enough "information scent" — no more.

    Title-only is too little; a data dump is too much. The Home sweet spot is a cover, title, format icon, and one or two key signals (credit hours, ★ rating, progress).

Caveat — shelves need discoverability cues on desktop

Shelf cards usually live in horizontal rows, and desktop users don't expect to scroll sideways. Peek the next card off the right edge, keep arrows always visible, and pair every row with a "See all →" link. See the Carousel rules below.

What we recommend

  • Home: shelf-style compact cards for Recommended, Jump Back In, What's New.
  • Catalog & search: large data-rich cards, where price/credits/rating drive a decision.
  • Standardize the compact card's meta (format icon · one key stat · rating).

Open question · Decision 🔗Copy link

And on the Recommended for You page — Home’s shelf cards, or the Catalog’s detailed cards?

Recommendation

Use the Course Catalog cards on the Recommended for You page. It’s a destination with browse-to-decide intent — functionally a personalized catalog — so it belongs with the Catalog, not with the Home band that sent the learner there. Detail then escalates across the three surfaces, and we maintain two card styles instead of three.

Context

The entry above settles Home vs. Catalog. It leaves out the third surface: the Recommended for You page — the Explore-rail section reached from the rail or from the band’s “See All →”. Both treatments are already built and flag-switchable (recommended-page-cards: Shelf cards, the compact squares that match Home, vs. Course Catalog cards). Worth noting that the flag’s own description already states MVP ships the Catalog cards — this entry records why, and flags that the committed default is still shelf and needs to be flipped to match the intent.

The three-tier model

  • Home “Recommended for you” band → shelf cards. Re-entry, zero commitment. The learner came to check progress and resume something; the band is a peripheral offer competing with the Current Learning Path. Its only job is to earn a click — a cover, a title, and one meta cue (format · rating, or price for a non-member) does that.
  • Recommended for You page → Course Catalog cards. A destination, browse-to-decide. This is where the click gets qualified, so the decision inputs belong on the card face.
  • Course Catalog → Course Catalog cards. Search and compare. Price, credit hours, format, rating, and state applicability are the task.

Why — the reasoning

  1. Card detail should track the decision the surface actually asks for.

    Price and credit hours are decision inputs, so they earn their space where a decision is being made and waste it where one isn’t. On Home that detail also costs real vertical space above the fold, pushing the learner’s own progress down for an offer they didn’t come for.

  2. A destination has to look like it earned the click.

    If “See All →” lands the learner on cards identical to the ones they just left, the click reads as unrewarded — the page feels like more of the same rather than a step forward. Escalating the detail is the cheapest signal that the navigation went somewhere.

  3. Comparison across a click is expensive.

    On a destination page the learner is building a shortlist. Strip the meta and every candidate costs a click to evaluate, forcing them to hold the comparison in memory — or to abandon the page for the Catalog, which does show it.

  4. Two card styles are cheaper to keep honest than three.

    Sharing one component between the Recommended page and the Catalog means one place to change when the meta row, tier badging, or price treatment moves. A bespoke middle tier would drift.

Tradeoff — tall cards and horizontal shelves fight each other

The Catalog cards are much taller, so each shelf row grows and fewer shelves fit above the fold — losing the “many rows, many angles” benefit that makes the shelf pattern worth having. Either accept 2–3 shelves above the fold, or move that page to a filtered grid with the category as a filter control rather than a row.

What we recommend

  • Home band: shelf cards — unchanged.
  • Recommended for You page: the Course Catalog cards; flip the recommended-page-cards committed default from shelf to large so the default matches the documented MVP intent.
  • Catalog: unchanged.
  • Decide the shelf-vs-grid question on the Recommended page before the taller cards ship, not after.

How we’d validate this

This entry is reasoned, not yet tested — it’s a defensible hypothesis about intent, not a finding. What would settle it: a moderated usability test, 5–8 learners (run both member and non-member), starting on Home with a real renewal task — “you have 6 ethics hours left before your license expires — find a course that counts.” Watch where the shortlist actually gets built.

  • Confirms the model: learners shortlist on the Recommended page and can state a course’s price and credit hours before opening anything; non-members register the price without a click.
  • Disconfirms it: learners treat the page as a waypoint and leave for the Catalog to compare — meaning the page needs Catalog affordances (filters, sort), not just Catalog cards.
  • Also disconfirms it: the taller cards leave so little visible that a learner reads one-and-a-half shelves as “that’s all there is” — the illusion-of-completeness failure, which argues for the grid.
  • Instrumentation gap — read before quoting any A/B: the live card A/B (home-recommended-card-ab) is presentation-only — it does not bucket learners or log exposure, so it cannot declare a winner. Its two arms (Compact square vs. What’s Trending) are also both low-detail Home treatments, so it doesn’t test this question at all. Settling it quantitatively needs a shelf-vs-Catalog-card arm on the page, plus impression / card-click / sheet-open / enroll events.

What are the rules of the Carousel component?

Rule of thumb

Use a carousel only for a short, related set the learner browses at their own pace — and put them in control. No auto-advance without a pause control, never bury important content in later slides, always signal there's more off-screen. If a set is large or every item matters, use a grid or list.

Context

We use carousels in the Recommended / Jump Back In shelves and the What's New featured row. Worth noting: our existing What's New featured carousel already follows the core rule — manual prev/next, no auto-advance or timers — so this mostly codifies what we already do right.

When to use a carousel — and when not to

  • Use it for a short, thematically-related set of roughly equal priority.
  • Don't make it the only path to important content — that needs a non-carousel home too.
  • Don't use one for a large set — past ~a dozen items, switch to a list or grid.

Motion & controls

  • Default to no auto-advance. Manual control beats auto-rotation; most users never engage past the first slide.
  • If it must auto-rotate, it must have a visible Pause/Stop and pause on hover/focus (WCAG 2.2.2, motion > 5s).
  • Always-visible arrows (not hover-only) plus swipe on touch; large targets, never tiny dots as the only control.
  • Respect prefers-reduced-motion.

Discoverability

  • Peek the next item off the right edge — the strongest "there's more" cue.
  • Treat dots as decoration, not the signifier.
  • Keep the set reachable in ~3–4 steps and pair with a "See all →" link.

Accessibility

  • Fully keyboard operable; use the ARIA carousel pattern and announce slide changes politely.
The bigger question — do we even need a carousel?

Carousels consistently underperform static content. Before reaching for one, ask whether a controllable shelf or a plain grid does the job better. Reserve the auto-rotating banner for low-stakes marketing.

Decided · Ported from review 🔗Copy link

Should the section eyebrow labels ("LEARNING PATH", "WHAT'S NEW") be made more prominent?

Decision

No — keep the eyebrows subordinate. Their job is to orient the eye into the important content (the pathway title, the CTA), not to be the focal point. Amplifying them flattens the hierarchy that points learners at what matters.

Why — the reasoning

Eyebrow text sits physically at the top but occupies a secondary place in the information hierarchy — position and emphasis are different axes. Design guidance is explicit that an eyebrow's visual weight "must be subordinated to the headline," is typically 12–14px, and exists for "context and categorization, never to convey critical information." Keeping it small is what lets it work: the eye catches the little label first for a one-word orientation ("What's New"), then hands off to the larger, higher-contrast title and CTA. Enlarging it to match its top position breaks that hand-off and makes the label compete with the content it introduces.

Component standard · Rules 🔗Copy link

How should the NEW tag be used on cards?

Standard

Use it selectively. A NEW tag is useful only if it stays rare. Its single biggest risk is overuse — the moment it's on most cards, it stops being a signal.

The rules (apply to every card badge)

  • Cap how much of a set is tagged NEW at once — roughly the top 10–15%.
  • Time-limit it (e.g. 30–60 days) so it ages off on its own instead of becoming permanent wallpaper.
  • Never stack or duplicate — at most one meaningful badge beyond the resource-type label.
  • Keep it secondary to the cover image and title.
  • Only badge a clear, uncommon status — if a badge applies to most items, it isn't a signal. If it doesn't change whether a learner clicks, drop it.

Decided · Ported from review 🔗Copy link

Can the hero support a photo background with a color / opacity overlay?

Decision

Yes — but constrain it. Support a photo background with a fixed scrim (and/or a small palette of contrast-validated overlay colors). Fully open photo + open color customization can't guarantee legible text, so ship the constrained MVP first and expand later.

Why — the reasoning

The overlay is the mechanism that keeps text legible on a photo. WCAG 1.4.3 requires 4.5:1 for normal text (3:1 for large), and that applies over images just as over a solid fill. The complication: contrast varies across a photo — a headline readable over a dark region can vanish over a bright one. The recommended fix is exactly the requested scrim: a semi-transparent layer at roughly 20–40% opacity that quiets the background into a consistent, high-contrast surface. Because an uploaded photo can be anything, a fixed scrim guarantees legibility with far less engineering and QA than open customization.

Open question · Data-viz 🔗Copy link

How should the dashboard show course completion — a donut / gauge, or a bar?

Recommendation

Default to a linear / stacked bar for progress; use a half-donut gauge only as an at-a-glance hero flourish, always paired with the exact number. Never let the arc alone carry the value — a ring looks richer but reads less precisely than a bar.

Context

The Courses widget explored three chart shapes: a tri-segment donut (Variant B), a half-donut gauge (Option 4), and a horizontal stacked bar (Option 2). All show the same thing — how many courses are completed / in-progress / not started — in a widget that has to sit in the tight 3-up dashboard row.

Why — the reasoning

  1. People read length more accurately than angle or area.

    A bar's filled length maps straight to a percentage, so learners judge "how far along" precisely and can compare segments at a glance. Rings and gauges ask the eye to judge angle/arc, which is measurably less accurate — the classic perceptual finding behind "prefer bars for values you need to read."

  2. Gauges are for "red-light / green-light," not comparison or precision.

    Gauge charts shine for a single metric against a target at a glance, but they "consume significant real estate for a single value," and lining several up "makes comparisons hard to read." For a status mix in a compact widget, a stacked bar is more efficient.

  3. Always show the number.

    Whatever the shape, put the exact count / percent in or beside it — the chart is the glance, the number is the truth. A stacked bar has the bonus of showing the whole mix (completed · in-progress · not started) in one 12px strip.

What we recommend

  • Default the Courses / progress widgets to a horizontal stacked bar (mix) or linear progress (single value) — compact enough for the 3-up row and precise.
  • A half-donut gauge is fine as a single hero KPI when space allows and it's paired with the number in the well — not for comparing segments.
  • Build it through our data-viz system — consistent palette, legible in light and dark, and segments labeled so meaning isn't carried by color alone (see Foundations · Accessibility).

Decided · Ported from decision doc 🔗Copy link

Should external website links (Blog, Podcast, Community) live in the primary left nav?

Recommendation

No — keep the primary rail internal. Move external destinations (Blog, Appraisal Podcast, Community) to a footer/utility zone or a clearly separated "Community & More" cluster. Reserve the left nav for destinations that keep the learner inside the platform.

Why — the reasoning

  1. Primary navigation should stay within one consistent context.

    Established guidance is that primary nav should only link to destinations that share the same navigational context. The moment a link lands on a page where the nav is different — or gone — the learner loses orientation and their way back. External sites don't carry our nav.

  2. Don't mix content destinations with utility links.

    Interleaving high-level destinations (Learning Paths, Courses, Certificates) with ancillary links (a blog, a podcast, a social community) makes the rail harder to scan. Today's "Explore" group does exactly this.

  3. Silent hand-offs violate expectations and accessibility guidance.

    Leaving the site with no warning is disorienting for everyone and a barrier for AT users. WCAG-aligned practice is to explicitly signal external / new-tab destinations — a burden easier to avoid by relocating them.

  4. External rows dilute our highest-value in-app paths.

    Every row competes for attention. Offsite links sitting shoulder-to-shoulder with conversion paths like "Become a Member" dilute the paths we most want learners to take.

What we recommend

  • Keep the rail internal — in-app destinations only.
  • Relocate external destinations to a footer/utility zone or a separated "Community & More" cluster.
  • If any external link must stay, signal it — visible external/new-tab indicator with an accessible name; never a silent hand-off.
  • Protect conversion paths — "Become a Member" reads as a first-class in-app destination.

Component standard · Explored 🔗Copy link

How should the active / selected nav item be indicated?

Standard

Use a multi-channel selected state — an accent bar + weight + background fill — and expose it programmatically with aria-current. Never rely on color alone to say "you are here."

Why — the reasoning

Six selected-state options were explored (accent left-bar, wash, tint, solid fill, pill, icon-accent). The principle across all of them: the active row needs redundant cues — a shape/position change (the left accent bar), a weight change (bold label), and a background fill — so the state survives for color-blind users and low-contrast conditions. Color-only selection fails WCAG 1.4.1 (Use of Color). And the state must be machine-readable: aria-current="page" so assistive tech announces the current location, not just a visual tint.

Accessibility

Cross-cutting WCAG 2.1 / 2.2 AA standards that apply to every page, distilled from our dashboard-rebrand accessibility audits. Fix these at the token / shared-component level and they propagate everywhere.

Standard · From audit 🔗Copy link

Every interactive control needs a visible focus indicator.

Standard

Ship an explicit :focus-visible ring on all custom controls — including the rail nav rows, profile button, and expanders. Custom-painted buttons on the dark nav surface can't rely on the browser default ring; its visibility is inconsistent across OS/browser.

Why / where

WCAG 2.4.7 (Focus Visible) requires a visible keyboard-focus indicator. Our codebase already has the convention (.cre-learning-path-card:focus-visible, table rows, links) — the rail is the one high-traffic control that opts out, so keyboard users can lose their place in the primary navigation. Mirror the existing ring pattern via a scoped token rule.

Standard · From audit 🔗Copy link

Accent text on dark surfaces must swap to lighter ramp stops.

Standard

Drive contrast-aware color through tokens. Dark mode re-skins surfaces to each brand's deep-navy ramp but often leaves accent text (primary / secondary / cta / success) on its original mid-ramp stops, which then collapse in contrast. On dark surfaces, accent text should use the lighter stops (*-200 / *-300).

Why / where

WCAG 1.4.3 (4.5:1 text) and 1.4.11 (3:1 non-text) apply in every theme. A precedent already exists — ExploreLink is contrast-aware (light vs. dark stop-swap) — and should be generalized to eyebrows, inline links, status pills, stat values, tags, and tile type-labels rather than patched per component, so all brands benefit at once.

Standard · From audit 🔗Copy link

Never encode meaning in color alone.

Standard

Every state carries a second cue. Status, selection, validity, and chart series must be distinguishable without relying on color — pair color with an icon, text label, shape, or weight.

Why / where

WCAG 1.4.1 (Use of Color) requires that color isn't the only means of conveying information. This governs status pills (Completed / Expired / In Progress), the selected nav state (see Navigation), certificate validity, and any data-viz that colors by series. The team has already done deliberate color-independence work; this entry makes it the explicit bar for new surfaces.

Respect reduced motion; no auto-advancing motion without control.

Standard

Honor prefers-reduced-motion, and never auto-move content without a control. Non-essential animation is dropped for users who ask for less motion; any auto-advancing content needs a pause/stop/hide affordance. Our carousels are manual by standard.

Why / where

WCAG 2.2.2 (Pause, Stop, Hide) covers any motion that starts automatically and runs more than 5 seconds; WCAG 2.3.3 (Animation from Interactions) says non-essential motion should be disable-able. The shell already handles prefers-reduced-motion and ships no auto-advancing carousels — this entry locks that in as the standard so new components inherit it (see the Carousel rules on Home).

Learning Path

Where a learner sees the pathway they're on and switches between the paths they hold. Decisions here are about how "all my paths" is surfaced — a quick in-context sheet vs. a full destination — and how the list holds up as the number of paths grows.

Open question · Decision 🔗Copy link

Should "all my learning paths" open in a slide-over sheet, or navigate to a full page?

Recommendation

Both — matched to the job. A slide-over sheet for quick, in-context switching from the dashboard; a full Learning Paths page for browsing and managing. Keep the sheet lightweight but scalable (search + status chips + sort) so it holds up even at 40+ paths.

Context

The dashboard's Current Learning Path widget shows a "View All (N)" link; clicking it opens the My Learning Paths slide-over — a pinned Selected Path, a search field, status chips (Expiring soon in yellow, Expired in red), and a Sort control. A learner may hold one path, a few, or 40+ (the learning-paths-count flag models one / realistic / many / lots), so both surfaces have to survive scale.

Why — the reasoning

  1. A slide-over keeps context.

    An overlay lets a learner act — switch path, peek at status — without losing the dashboard behind it. That's the right tool for a quick, high-frequency task; a full page navigation is heavier than the job needs.

  2. But a sheet must scale or degrade gracefully.

    At 40+ paths, a bare list in a sheet becomes a scroll-hunt. That's exactly why the sheet bakes in search, status chips, and sort. When the task shifts from "switch" to "compare / manage many," that's the full page's job.

  3. Don't make the sheet the only home.

    The full Learning Paths page (grid / list / table) is the durable, linkable, sortable destination; the sheet is the express lane off the dashboard. Both exist; neither replaces the other.

What we recommend

  • Dashboard "View All" → slide-over for quick switching, with search / filter / sort built in.
  • Full Learning Paths page for management, with the sortable table view (below) for scale.

Component standard · Decision 🔗Copy link

When paths get numerous, what makes the list scannable?

Recommendation

Add a true, sortable table view alongside grid and list. Click-to-sort columns, status pills that aren't color-only, whole-row click targets, and dates formatted for scanning — mirroring the canonical My Courses table so behavior doesn't fork.

Why — the reasoning

  1. Tables win when the task is compare / find across many rows.

    Once a learner has many paths, the job is triage on shared attributes — title, status, hours, license expiry, progress. Sortable columns let them pull "expiring soonest" or "least progress" to the top, which cards and lists can't do as directly.

  2. Mirror the existing My Courses table.

    Reusing the same chrome, tokens, and patterns means learners transfer knowledge across surfaces and we maintain one table behavior, not two. The table view is a third mode on the existing grid / list toggle, and the choice persists.

  3. Accessibility carries over.

    Semantic <table>, status meaning not by color alone (pills with text labels), adequate row target height, and a horizontal-scroll wrapper on small screens — the Foundations standards apply here directly.

Membership

Every surface whose appearance depends on what the learner has purchased — gated content, purchase sheets, pricing, and upgrade prompts. Decisions here govern how we ask for the upgrade and how price adapts to the learner's tier.

Open question · Decision 🔗Copy link

What upsell CTA pattern should gated / purchase sheets use?

Recommendation

Lead with value, then one clear primary action. Show the learner what they get — the course and what membership unlocks — before the ask. One primary CTA per state (Enroll / Add to Cart / Upgrade), honest pricing with a "$0 with membership" savings framing, and no stack of competing buttons.

Context

We explored purchase-sheet upsell layouts (options A / B) for the product sheet — the moment a non-member or lower-tier learner hits a paid or gated item.

Why — the reasoning

  1. Show value before the ask.

    Paywall research is consistent: conversion improves when the screen first demonstrates the benefit (the content itself, what a membership unlocks). A cold "pay now" with no value framing underperforms.

  2. One primary CTA.

    Competing, equally-weighted buttons raise decision time and dilute the path. Make the desired action primary and let secondary options (e.g. "See plans") recede.

  3. Be transparent about price — and frame it.

    Show the real price alongside the savings framing ("$0 with membership" / "Save $X with Passport") rather than hiding it. Transparency builds trust and makes membership the obvious value.

  4. Trigger at the moment of intent.

    Surface the upsell on the locked / premium item the learner just reached — contextual, not a random interstitial — so the ask lands when motivation is highest.

Decided · From plan 🔗Copy link

How should pricing display adapt to the learner's membership tier?

Decision

Model entitlement as (product × tier), not member × non-member. Resolve every product to exactly one of four commerce states — included, priced-teaser, priced-upsell, locked — each with a defined price slot and a single CTA. One resolver, one source of truth, so cards and sheets never disagree.

Context

Today the product sheet branches on a single boolean, isMemberAccess — members see "Included," everyone else sees a price. That binary can't express "a Lite member still pays for a Passport-only course." The tier model already exists in AccountContext; what's missing is per-product entitlement data (includedFromTier, memberExclusive) plus one resolver that turns (product, tier) into a display state.

The four states

included

Product is $0 at the viewer's tier

  • Price slot: "Included with your membership"
  • CTA: Enroll
priced-teaser

Non-member, buyable à la carte

  • Price + "$0 with membership" badge
  • CTA: Add to Cart
priced-upsell

Member, but item is a higher tier

  • Price + "$0 with Passport" / "Unlock with Passport"
  • CTA: Add to Cart / Upgrade
locked

Member-exclusive, not buyable

  • "Member exclusive" (no price)
  • CTA: See membership plans

Why — the reasoning

  1. A binary can't express tiered reality.

    Real entitlement depends on the product's lowest-including tier and whether it's buyable at all. A member / non-member flag collapses that and produces wrong CTAs for mid-tier learners.

  2. Consistency across surfaces is the whole point.

    A single resolver feeding cards and sheets guarantees the same state, price slot, and CTA everywhere — the shared entitlement-states taxonomy, not per-surface guesses.

  3. Framing turns price into an upsell.

    "$0 with Passport" and "Unlock with Passport" make the tier delta legible and motivate the upgrade without hiding cost.

Should the Progress Tracker and What's New share one connected hero, or be visually separated?

Archived · Aug 6, 2026 — removed from the active project.

Decision

Separate them. Two distinct jobs — "where am I in my learning" vs. "what's new to explore" — deserve two distinct cards. The connected hero treatment blended them so What's New couldn't stand out; the layout reverts to the original separated design.

Why — the reasoning

Grouping is a signal: elements placed together read as one thing. When the Progress Tracker and What's New share a single hero surface, proximity tells the eye they're a unit, which flattens the boundary between a personal status widget and a content-discovery module. Separating them lets each own its own visual hierarchy and headline, and lets What's New carry the emphasis (pathway title + "Start the pathway" CTA) it's supposed to.

This was a fast agreement with the stakeholder (Diana), so it needed little external research — but it rests on the Gestalt principle of proximity: things that are separate in meaning should be separate in space.

Should we add a "WHAT'S NEW" tag on top of the "WHAT'S NEW" eyebrow?

Archived · Aug 6, 2026 — removed from the active project.

Decision

No. The eyebrow already labels the section; a tag restating it is redundant and spends the section's limited "salience budget" on nothing. A tag is only worth adding when it carries distinct, meaningful information — a status like "Coming Soon" or "Member Exclusive."

Why — the reasoning

Design-system guidance says indicators that don't add significant information should be dropped, not styled — using them anyway causes "cognitive overload." Badges win attention through pre-attentive features (color, shape, position); that only works when they're rationed. When everything carries a badge, none of them pops. And stacking multiple emphasized chips in one header (e.g. "NEW THIS MONTH" + "Passport") is the classic competing-focal-points failure — three levels of emphasis is about all people can discern. Rule: at most one meaningful badge per section header, beyond the resource-type label.

Should cards show the resource type (Course / Podcast / AI Tool)?

Archived · Aug 6, 2026 — removed from the active project.

Decision

Yes — and it already follows an existing UX standard. The resource type appears on both the small and large card variants. Showing type sets the learner's expectation before they click.

Why — the reasoning

A type label is information scent: it lets a learner recognize what a tile is (a 45-minute course vs. a podcast episode vs. an AI tool) without opening it — recognition over recall. It's a small, meaningful signal, which is exactly the kind of label that earns its place on a card (unlike the redundant "new" tag above). The type label is distinct from promotional badges and doesn't count against the one-badge rule.

What color should the left-nav rail be?

Archived · Aug 6, 2026 — removed from the active project.

Direction

A brand-neutral rail, validated for contrast across every brand and theme. Three treatments were explored — V1 navy (#17233F), V2 graphite (#2B2D31), V3 light neutral (#E3E6EB). All keep the rail hue-neutral so each brand's color lives in the content, and all must pass AA in every brand × light/dark × member/non-member combination.

Why — the reasoning

A neutral rail is a deliberate hierarchy choice: the chrome recedes so brand color and content can lead. It also keeps one nav implementation working across all brands instead of a per-brand colored rail. The constraint that governs the pick is contrast — the rail's text, icons, and selected state must hold up in light and dark and for member and non-member variants. A light-neutral rail (V3) must invert to a dark neutral in dark mode so it doesn't glare. The dashboard-rebrand a11y audit confirms the shipped rail passes AA across all five brands × light/dark × member/non-member.
