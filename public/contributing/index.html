Part 0What this is

This page gets you from nothing to your first link on the Hub. Read it once. After that, Part 4 is the only part you need, and it is three sentences long.

There are three things to understand, and the rest of this page is detail about them.

The Hub

A website where everyone — designers, developers, stakeholders — sees the team's work. It is six pages: three boards of links (Design, Research, PRDs), the developer handoffs (Development, Done) and the Archive.

Your branch

Your own private copy of the project. You change it as much as you like. Nothing on it is seen by anyone until you share it, and nothing on it changes the product until Jill ships it.

Claude

The desktop app you type sentences to. It does every technical step for you — copying the project, saving your work, publishing it — so you never type a command.

You design. Claude does the technical parts. Jill ships.You never type a command, you never run the website on your own computer, and you never touch the copy of the project that everyone sees.

Part 1The whole thing in one picture

Your work travels left to right. Every box is a place; every arrow is one sentence you say to Claude, or one paste.

In words, left to right:

  1. You change the project in a folder on your computer, by talking to Claude.
  2. You say "share my work". Claude checks it, saves it, sends your branch to GitHub, and hands you one web address.
  3. That address is your branch, live. Netlify builds it a few minutes after every share. You never run the site yourself.
  4. You paste the address on the Hub's Design page. The moment you do, everyone with the Hub's link can open it.
  5. The team looks and talks. You change, share again; the address stays the same.
  6. Jill ships it. She puts your branch into the product. The Demo build link at the foot of the Hub then shows it, and you start the next piece.
Most Important Thing. You work on YOUR BRANCH. Never push directly to main."Share my work" never touches main. Main is the copy everyone sees, and only Jill puts anything there. If you ever find yourself on main, "share my work" moves you off it — and if anything looks wrong, stop and tell Jill.

Part 3Setting up your computer

Once, on your first day. Four sentences to Claude, each with a copy button when you hover it.

  1. Connect the folder.

    Open the Claude desktop app, go to the Code tab, and choose the empty folder you made. From now on, Claude can read and change what is in it.

  2. Copy the project down.
    YOUClone the GitHub repository jillmoreno/xcel-ux-dashboard into this folder.

    "Clone" means copy. A minute later the folder is full of the project's files. You will not read them; Claude will.

  3. Install what the project needs.
    YOUInstall the project's dependencies.

    The project leans on a few hundred small pieces of code other people wrote. This fetches them. A few minutes; you only do it once, and Claude will tell you if it ever needs doing again.

  4. Put your name on your work.
    YOUFor this project, set my git name to Your Name and my git email to you@scorebuilders.com.

    Use your real name and your work email. Every change you save carries this name, so the team can see whose it is — and "share my work" names your branch after it.

  5. Check.
    YOUWhich branch am I on, and is the project up to date?

    Claude should say you are on main and up to date. That is fine for now: the first time you say "share my work", it moves you onto a branch of your own.

Nothing here runs the website. You will never start the site on your own computer, and you do not need to. Your preview is the branch link in Part 1, which Netlify makes for you.

Part 4The three sentences

Everything after setup is one of these. Two are yours; the third is Jill's.

  1. Share my work.
    YOUShare my work.

    Claude makes you a branch if you need one, checks the work, saves it, sends it up, and answers with one line: the address to paste on the Hub's Design page. It asks you nothing. If a check fails it says what, in one line, and stops.

  2. Write dev handoff notes.
    YOUWrite dev handoff notes for the study streak card.

    Claude reads what you built and asks you six short questions in plain words — what it is made of, its states, its buttons, the edge cases, what you decided, anything else. It writes the page developers read, on the Hub's Development page, and asks for your Design link so the two point at each other.

  3. Ship. (Jill's)
    JILLShip Anjani's study streak card.

    Jill puts a branch into the product. Claude shows her every switch the branch would change, she answers yes or no to each, and the Demo build then shows the work. Nobody else can do this — GitHub refuses, by design.

No slash commands to remember. The plain sentence is the command. (They exist for people who like them — /share-my-work, /dev-handoff-notes, /ship — but you never need one.)

Part 6The one rule: switches

Every change to the product sits behind a switch. That one idea is what lets several designers work on one product without anyone's half-finished work appearing in the Demo build.

  1. On your branch, the switch is on. Your branch link shows your work.
  2. On main, the switch is off. The product looks exactly as it did.
  3. When Jill ships your branch, she decides the switch. Claude lists every switch the branch would change and she answers yes or no to each. Then it asks whether a switch that has been decided should be retired, so the code stays clean — the version that won becomes the thing, and the other one is kept on the Archive page.

You get all of this by asking for it in the same breath as the work:

YOUBuild it behind a feature flag that is on by default on this branch.

Then ask "What is the flag called?" and write it down. Reviewers can flip any switch live on your build from the robot icon on the dark bar, so you can show both versions from one address.

Changing something that already exists? Do not edit it. Ask Claude to copy it to a sibling and build on the copy, with a switch choosing between the two. That way you and the person editing the original never collide.

Part 7Your first change, start to finish

Set up and ready? This is the loop from Part 1 with the actual sentences.

  1. Say what you want, the way you would to a developer beside you.

    Name the page, the component, what it should do instead. Ask for the switch (Part 6). If Claude names a file you do not recognise, ask "What is that file?" before saying yes.

  2. Share it.
    YOUShare my work.

    Claude answers with one address.

  3. Paste it on Design.

    Open the Hub, Design, Add link. Paste the address, give it a title, write one line about where to look, pick your name. Done — the team can open it.

  4. Look at it the way a reviewer would.

    Open your row on the Hub and click through from the page a reviewer would start on, not straight to your component. Things that are invisible on a direct link are obvious on the first click.

  5. Keep going.

    Each further change is step 1 and then "share my work" again; the address stays the same, so the row on Design stays right. When the next change is really a different piece of work, say "start a fresh branch for the next piece" and the next share makes a new one.

That is a chunk. Finish a chunk, share it, start fresh for the next. Small branches are the habit the whole Hub is built around.

Part 8Things never to do

Short, because Claude refuses most of them for you and says why.

  • Never push to main. "Share my work" never does. You cannot do it by accident: Claude refuses the command and says whose step it is, and GitHub refuses the push even if something slipped past Claude.
  • Never run the site on your own computer. Your branch link is the preview.
  • Never edit the Hub's own files. The project list, the demo bar, the routines. Claude refuses and names the file; ask Jill.
  • Never put review pages in public/prototypes/. A standalone page goes in public/demos/.
  • Never invent a number. A fee, a lesson count, a date XCEL has not published is marked as invented in the code and on screen. Ask instead.
  • Never share the Demo build as your work. Send your branch link. The Demo build shows what is already decided.

You work on your branch. Jill works on main. The one rule under every word above — it is the stop sign in Part 1.
